# Participatory discrepancies

*Following the threads from a family conversation, and finding that they are one
thread. Exploratory — descriptive rather than prescriptive, and it ends in a
disagreement rather than a conclusion.*

---

## Keil already named it, in 1987

Charles Keil, *Participatory Discrepancies and the Power of Music*, *Cultural
Anthropology* 2(3):

> The power of music is in its participatory discrepancies, and these are
> basically of two kinds: **processual** and **textural**.

**Processual** discrepancies are timing: the slight delays and anticipations
within a drummer's beat, between members of a rhythm section, between soloist and
accompaniment. What gets called swing, groove, drive, push.

**Textural** discrepancies are pitch and timbre: the out-of-tuneness and tonal
grain that invite participation. Keil's examples include **paired instruments
producing blended harmonics**, and the wobbly vibrato of electric guitar.

That second category is the **blending** that came up in conversation — attention
to certain sounds while playing, two things not quite the same becoming one thing
with life in it. Keil had a name for it thirty-nine years ago, and put it in the
same frame as the timing.

His account of *why* it works is the part that matters most here: these things
operate largely **outside conscious rational analysis**, their meanings remain
"various and ambiguous," and that ambiguity is what gives them "active
participatory power." The listener is drawn into physical and emotional
involvement rather than into analysing syntax.

And on notation, in a sentence that could sit beside Seeger's first hazard:
written notation captures only a **"petrified skeleton"** of music-making.

## The threads converge, and they were never separate

| From the conversation | Where it lands |
|---|---|
| participatory discrepancies | Keil 1987, the frame for all of it |
| blending, attention while playing | Keil's *textural* discrepancies, explicitly |
| polyrhythms in Angine de Poitrine | processual, at the level of whole parts |
| Adam Neely & Shawn Crowder playing with timing | processual, done deliberately and on purpose |
| Lascabettes's geometry of rhythm | the representation that *cannot* show either |
| Free Play | the disposition that lets discrepancy happen |
| improvisation for classical musicians | the training that removes permission for it |

And a thread from much earlier in this work: **the collaboration nut that never
cracked.** If the power is in the discrepancies, then networked music tools have
been solving precisely the wrong half — they quantise, snap, align and sync,
which is to say they remove the thing. What is easy in person is not the audio
path. It is being slightly, mutually, productively off.

That is a hypothesis, not a finding. But it is a better hypothesis than latency.

## The complication, which is the interesting part

**Keil's claim is contested, and by exactly the kind of research this project
says it respects.** Several controlled studies have tested whether microtiming
deviations increase rated groove, and repeatedly found that they do not — and
sometimes that quantised versions are rated groovier:

- Frühauf, Kopiez & Platz, *The Effect of Microtiming Deviations on the Perception
  of Groove in Short Rhythms*
- Davies, Madison, Silva & Gouyon, on the same question
- Senn et al., *The Effect of Expert Performance Microtiming on Listeners'
  Experience of Groove in Swing or Funk Music* (Frontiers, 2016)
- Datseris et al., *Microtiming Deviations and Swing Feel in Jazz* (*Scientific
  Reports*, 2019)
- Câmara et al. (2023), and a *Revisiting Charles Keil* commentary in *Empirical
  Musicology Review* — the argument is still live

**Two claims are being run together, and they should not be.**

1. **Keil's**: expressive *deviations from a norm* create participation and power.
2. **Polak & London's**: in Mande drumming the subdivision is **categorically
   non-isochronous** — 41:31:28, 59:41 — and Long/Short are *discrete metrical
   categories*, not deviations from anything.

The lab studies test (1). They do not touch (2). A pattern whose underlying
structure is uneven is not a pattern with expressive deviation applied to an even
one, and quantising it does not "remove feel" — it **changes the meter**. Keeping
these apart matters for anything built on rings and grids, because the second
claim is the one that bites on representation and it is not in dispute.

So the state of play, honestly: the structural claim is solid; the expressive
claim is beloved, intuitively obvious to players, and has not survived controlled
testing well. That is an unusually good thing to hand a room.

## Which makes a session, and it needs almost nothing

A recording, a DAW or any quantise button, and people.

- Play something with obvious feel. Quantise it. Play both.
- Ask which one grooves. Do not say which is which.
- Take the vote. Then say that the published experiments mostly agree with
  whichever side lost, and that the argument has been running since 1987.
- Then play a Mande recording and try to place the grid on it — and find that you
  cannot, because the subdivision is not even. A different problem entirely.

This passes every disposition in `docs/05`. Nobody can be wrong. The facilitator
does not know the answer either — nobody does. It is *enquiring* in the literal
sense, and it is the first session in this repo where the participants' responses
are genuinely data rather than a teaching device.

Adam Neely and Shawn Crowder's timing experiments in Sungazer are the same
exercise done by people who can play it, and would make good listening material
for the same session.

## Lascabettes, and the representation that cannot show this

Paul Lascabettes — postdoc at Strasbourg, PhD from IRCAM/Sorbonne, on the
editorial board of the *Journal of Mathematics and Music* — works on the discovery
of musical patterns and **the geometry of musical rhythms**, with an outreach
channel, *Mathémusique*, and an interactive site, **Rhythm Circle**
(rhythm-circle.com).

Two things follow. First, this is a working, public, French-language interactive
rhythm-geometry tool by a researcher who does outreach on purpose — which belongs
in the tool curation, and is the closest existing thing to the ring
representations already in the suite.

Second, and more usefully: **the geometry is exactly the representation that
cannot show a participatory discrepancy.** A ring places onsets at exact angles. A
41:31:28 subdivision can be drawn, if you know it; a drummer's push cannot. That
is not an objection to the geometry — it is the clearest available example of
`docs/02`'s whole argument, and it means the ring and the recording want to be in
the same room.

## Free Play, and permission

Nachmanovitch's *Free Play: Improvisation in Life and Art* (1990) sits underneath
all of this as the disposition rather than the mechanism. Discrepancy requires
someone willing to not be exact.

Which is why the improvisation-for-classical-musicians literature is relevant, and
it is sharper than expected:

- David Dolan et al., **"The Improvisational State of Mind"** (*Frontiers in
  Psychology*, 2018) — a multidisciplinary study of an improvisatory approach to
  classical repertoire.
- **"Are Classical Musicians Excluded from Improvisation? Cultural Hegemony and
  the Effects of Ideology on Musicians' Attitudes Towards Improvisation"**
  (*Contemporary Music Review*, 2021).
- **"Challenging score-centered norms in Western classical higher music
  education"** — an exploratory qualitative study of instructor-led initiatives in
  the UK and Europe.

That last title is this project's own position, appearing inside conservatoire
research. The translation work has a counterpart already doing it from the inside,
and citing them is cheaper than arguing from outside.

## What this is for

Not a curriculum. Possibly a session, definitely a thread. The reason it is worth
keeping amorphous a while longer is that participatory discrepancies touch every
register this project has been circling — rhythm, timbre, ensemble, notation's
limits, the failure of collaboration tools, the difference between playing and
executing — without resolving into any one of them. That is a good sign for a
concept and a bad sign for a curriculum, and for now the concept is what we want.
