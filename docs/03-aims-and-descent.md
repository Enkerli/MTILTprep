## Six kinds of aim

Sixteen items, and they are not one kind of thing. Some are capabilities a person walks out with, some are perceptual, some are frankly critical and social, one is explicitly about acquiring the conventional vocabulary. Sorting them is not a taxonomy to enforce — several sit in two places at once, and the overlaps are where the good sessions probably live. It is a way of noticing that "help people learn X" means something different in each row.

### Attending

*2 items*  

Noticing what is actually there, in sound you are already surrounded by.

- Unpacking what they like, noticing what makes it work
- Musical diversity in all its glory

*Session shape*  

Listening together to material the participants brought, with structured attention and no obligation to name.

*Evidence it worked*  

They hear something in a familiar track they had not heard before, and can point to it.

*Tools*  

Helpful. A spectrogram, a stem separator, an A/B switch. Representation earns its place here.

### Doing

*5 items*  

Capabilities someone leaves with — things they can now make happen.

- Learning a way to play with music which is more "intuitive" than most instruments
- Learning how to improvise
- Communicating through music
- Memorizing specific songs
- Expanding an improviser's "language"

*Session shape*  

Repeated practice with something responsive. These are the only aims that genuinely need return visits.

*Evidence it worked*  

They do the thing, later, unprompted, without you in the room.

*Tools*  

Central. This is where your suite lives, and "more intuitive than most instruments" is close to its design brief.

### Naming

*1 item*  

Access to a discourse community. The aim that is unapologetically about concepts.

- Learning the "lingo" to be able to talk with musicians

*Session shape*  

Experience first, then the word — and the word taught as a password into a room, not as the truth about the sound.

*Evidence it worked*  

They hold their own in a conversation with a musician and are not condescended to.

*Tools*  

Marginal but real. pitch-ontology is the right shape for this, provided it is used as a phrasebook rather than a syllabus.

### Situating

*4 items*  

Seeing music as made by people under conditions. Your discipline, unmediated.

- The context behind musical approaches
- Understanding the issues with how narrow musical learning tends to be
- How we've been commodifying music
- What musical tastes tell us about social groups (including "subcultures")

*Session shape*  

Discussion, comparison, primary material. Closer to a seminar or a listening club than to a workshop.

*Evidence it worked*  

They describe their own taste as situated rather than as a personality trait.

*Tools*  

Barely. No widget delivers this. A well-chosen pair of recordings does.

### Crossing

*2 items*  

Music as a way into something that isn't music — where the two look at the same object.

- The connections between music and other domains
- Acoustics as inspiration for certain musical practices or structures… with important modifications

*Session shape*  

One phenomenon, examined twice. The "important modifications" clause is the whole discipline of it — acoustics inspires, it does not explain.

*Evidence it worked*  

They can say where the analogy stops, not only where it holds.

*Tools*  

Helpful. The numeric and cyclic representations do real work here.

### Inhabiting

*2 items*  

A change in how a person carries themselves around music. The hardest to claim and possibly the most valuable.

- Casual musicking as liberating
- Singing as breathing exercise

*Session shape*  

Low stakes, repeated, no product. The session succeeds by not producing anything worth evaluating.

*Evidence it worked*  

They sing in the car. You will probably never find out.

*Tools*  

None. A tool in the room may actively harm this one by giving people something to be judged at.

## Two kinds out of six

## The descriptors are the pedagogy

You were right and I overread you. Thoresen's symbols and Sköld's staff are analytical and compositional apparatus; they are not connected to musical learning in any live way, and pointing at them does not make your statement less accurate. Timbre remains the thing that musical learning almost never represents.

But the descriptor question — the one you keep coming back to — turns out to have empirical ground under it, and the ground supports exactly the distinction you drew from Farnell. Across studies and across languages, three semantic dimensions recur, and they have acoustic correlates. "Cinematic" has none of this. "Bright" has all of it.

**Brightness**  
*bright · sharp · luminous · dull*  
Tracks spectral centroid — the amplitude-weighted mean frequency. Shifts in the spectral envelope are heard systematically as changes in brightness. **Texture**  
*rough · harsh · smooth*  
Tracks amplitude modulation within a critical band, roughly 15–300 Hz, plus high-frequency energy. This is your haptic dimension, already named as touch. **Fullness**  
*full · rich · thin · hollow*  
Tracks fundamental strength, inharmonicity and spectral variation over time. "Hollow" — one of yours — lives here.

Zacharakis and colleagues compared English and Greek speakers and found native language had very little effect on the configuration of the semantic space. Which means these three are not a Western convention you would be importing. They are close to a floor.

> **And the instabilities are the workshop material, not the problem**  
>
> - **Musicians rate more consistently than non-musicians.** The inconsistency of untrained descriptions is itself a finding worth showing people about themselves.
> - **The antonym pairs are suspect.** Semantic-differential work assumes dark–bright is a genuine opposition. It may not be. Ask a room and watch it fracture.
> - **"Soft" is polysemous** — it describes overall loudness and transient character, and people slide between the two without noticing.
> - **Some descriptors are instrument-specific.** Trombone reliably attracts emotion words that other brass don't. Your "woody" and "glassy" are material metaphors and probably behave this way.
> - **The known gap:** verbal descriptors fail at the sustained-versus-impulsive distinction, which is perceptually one of the biggest. There is a hole in language exactly where a workshop could point.

#### The functional descriptors are a different animal, and worth keeping separate

Bass, keys, pads, lead. These name a *role in an arrangement*, not a quality of sound — which is why they are stable where "cinematic" isn't, and why they organise soundware so effectively. They are closer to instrumentation than to timbre, and conflating the two is probably the single most common confusion in sample-library metadata.

#### Your information-architecture instinct and the descriptor pedagogy are the same project

A sample library's tags are folk descriptors at scale — an existing corpus of how people actually name sound, complete with disagreements. Curating one is ethnographic work you would be doing anyway, and it generates the material for the sessions. The library tool and the timbre pedagogy are one thing seen from two ends.

#### Syntorial versus Learning Synths, precisely

Syntorial sets a target patch and asks you to match it by ear; the exercise has a right answer. Learning Synths hands you a live synth in a browser and lets the explanation follow the play. You said you prefer discovery — the operative difference is the target, not the subject. And "known sounds" gives you the alternative to a target: you are not matching an arbitrary patch, you are chasing something you already love.

## Down to the single cycle, then back up

This is the most structurally interesting thing in your notes, and it may be the answer to the question you have been circling. It inverts the standard order: conventional curricula start at pitch and notation and never arrive at timbre. Yours starts at a sound somebody already loves and arrives at harmony last, if at all.

It is also where Schaeffer and Schafer meet without either having to be taught. The descent is reduced listening — you keep removing context until the sound stops being a recording *of* something. The ascent is ear cleaning in reverse. Schafer's *A Sound Education* gives a hundred exercises in the same register.

> **The single cycle**  
>
> The hinge, and the only moment in any music curriculum where a learner meets the smallest unit of sound as an object rather than as a note. Everything above it is built; everything below it was taken away.

#### Why this resolves the tools question

Your tools don't relate to MTILT because they have no order and no reason — each one is a good instrument with nothing telling a learner why they are holding it. The descent-and-ascent supplies both, and it costs nothing to build, because it is a *sequence*, not software. Five of the six ascent rungs already have a tool. The sixth is timbre, at the bottom, which is exactly where your gap is and exactly where the sequence spends its most interesting time.

#### Why the descent is the inclusive half

Nobody can be bad at it. Taking a recording apart requires no skill, no vocabulary and no instrument, and the material is something the learner chose. It is also the half that produces the descriptors — every step down is a moment where somebody has to say what they are hearing, in their own words, because none of the official ones apply yet.

#### The obvious first session

The descent alone, once, with two or three people and one track each. No ascent, no tools, no synthesis. Just down to the grain and a list of the words that came up. If that hour works, the rest of the structure has something to stand on. If it doesn't, you have learned the important thing early.
